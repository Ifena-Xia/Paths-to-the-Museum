1. Why Cultural Participation Matters

Cultural participation is not simply leisure. It is a form of learning, of cultural identification, and of civic life. Well-facilitated museum visits can produce measurable gains in children's science learning and reflective thinking, especially when an adult scaffolds the experience (Holmes 2011; Acosta and Haden 2022; Cronin et al. 2020), and early cultural involvement predicts lifelong engagement (Temple and Gan 2023). For some groups the museum is also a site of cultural validation: immigrant and minority visitors often cite heritage as a reason to attend (Paredes 2016). Receptive cultural activity even tracks health, with engagement associated with lower all-cause mortality (Løkken et al. 2020). In this sense museums work as vehicles of intergenerational transmission, and the question of who walks through the door is a question about how advantage is passed down.

2. What Shapes Participation, and What Restricts It

Across very different countries one predictor stands out: education, and especially parental education, is the most consistent predictor of cultural participation (Falk and Katz-Gerro 2016; Zieba 2017), often outweighing income or occupational class (Haag and Specht 2022; Fiorillo and Ofria 2024). The mechanism is cultural capital in Bourdieu's sense: the dispositions and competences that let a person decode and feel at ease in highbrow spaces, acquired at home and transmitted across generations (Bourdieu 1984; Bourdieu and Passeron 1977). Cultural capital is precisely what DiMaggio (1982) showed converts into participation and attainment, and parental education is its most common proxy.

The pattern reaches children directly. Out-of-school cultural participation is strongly predicted by parents' own engagement, neighborhood deprivation, and socioeconomic status (Mak and Fancourt 2021); school programs narrow the gap only temporarily, while family influence dominates the long run (Temple and Gan 2023). Class even shapes how families use museums, not only whether they visit: frequent attenders value curatorial expertise and learning, infrequent ones value rest areas and play (Borrione et al. 2021).

Geography compounds the divide. Rural residence, neighborhood deprivation, and thin infrastructure affect both access and a sense of cultural belonging (Mak et al. 2021; Rodríguez-Puello and Iturra 2024), and within households women's participation is further constrained by childcare and mobility (Ateca-Amestoy and Ugidos 2021). At the macro level, education explains more of the inequality in participation than race or geography, and the gap persists online rather than dissolving there, so cultural engagement stays clustered among the already-engaged (Ateca-Amestoy and Prieto-Rodriguez 2024).

3. The Case of China: Rapid Growth, Persistent Inequality

China's museum sector has ballooned. By the end of 2024 the country had 7,046 registered museums, roughly one per 200,000 people, more than nine in ten free to enter, and they recorded about 1.49 billion visits that year, a national record (National Cultural Heritage Administration, 2024). Admission fees have been swept away and public programming has expanded, even as cultural heritage is tied ever more tightly to state identity (Lu 2014; Shelach-Lavi 2019; Lin 2023, 2024). Yet who actually walks in remains sharply unequal. Maternal education predicts children's early cognitive stimulation, especially in cities (Rao et al. 2022), and even where entry is free, high-quality engagement is gated by design: limited reservation quotas, digital-first booking, and thin signage screen out families with less education or digital fluency (Li 2024). Recognition of museums' public value is itself concentrated among the highly educated (Zhao et al. 2024), which suggests participation is far from equitably distributed.

These barriers matter because Chinese museums now carry a double charge. They are state-sanctioned patriotic spaces and, at the same time, sites of experiential learning and social mobility. Yet large quantitative studies of children's attendance remain rare. Much work examines cultural nationalism, digital communication, or governance (Bollo and Zhang 2017); little asks how intergenerational inequality, and parental education in particular, shapes who reaches the museum at all. This study takes up that question.

4. Data and Measures

The analysis draws on the China Education Panel Survey (CEPS), which began in 2013 to 2014 with two cohorts, 7th and 9th grade. Using stratified sampling on local education levels and the size of the floating population, CEPS drew 28 county-level units, then randomly selected 112 schools and 438 classes, yielding 19,487 students. After removing cases with missing or internally inconsistent records, the analytic sample is 9,761 families.

What the outcome measures. The participation variable is the parent's report of how often the child visits "museums, zoos, science museums, and the like" over the past year. It therefore captures informal cultural and educational outings broadly, not art or history museums alone. Where the text says "museum participation," read it as this broader disposition. If zoos and science centers are less stratified by parental education than art or history museums, the parental-education effect estimated here is, if anything, a conservative lower bound for museums proper.

Participation frequency was coded on a 1 to 6 scale, from "never" to "more than once a week." Parental education is the higher of the two parents' schooling, in years. Several variables are binary: female, grade 9, has_siblings (one or more siblings, 49.7 percent), and intact home (both parents present, 48 percent). Urbanicity has three levels: urban core (36.3 percent), semi-urban (28.1 percent), and rural (35.6 percent).

Because China's hukou system often leaves children in their registered hometown while parents migrate, and because the survey records the left-behind arrangement for only 3,299 families, sibling counts are taken from the student's report regardless of co-residence, to avoid undercounting siblings in migrant families. The resulting measurement error is noted as a limitation.

5. OLS Model

Model 1 regresses participation frequency on the predictors without interactions:

\[ \widehat{\text{freq}} = 1.784 + 0.058\,\text{edu} - 0.123\,\text{semi} - 0.499\,\text{rural} + 0.056\,\text{female} - 0.186\,\text{grade9} - 0.122\,\text{siblings} + 0.062\,\text{intact} \]

Because the return to parental education may differ by place, Model 2 adds interactions between parental education and urbanicity:

\[ \widehat{\text{freq}} = 2.006 + 0.040\,\text{edu} - 0.477\,\text{semi} - 0.923\,\text{rural} + 0.029\,(\text{edu}\times\text{semi}) + 0.039\,(\text{edu}\times\text{rural}) + 0.056\,\text{female} - 0.181\,\text{grade9} - 0.125\,\text{siblings} + 0.066\,\text{intact} \]

An F-test prefers Model 2 (p = 0.0006): the interactions improve fit. The continuous education scale matters here, a point Section 8 returns to.

Predicted frequency falls as places become less urban, but the slope on parental education is steepest in rural areas, so a rural child whose parents are highly educated is predicted to participate nearly as often as an urban peer. Girls, 7th graders, and children in intact homes show somewhat higher predicted frequency. The sibling and intact-home coefficients should be read together, for reasons explained in Section 8.

6. Binary Logistic Model

The outcome is recoded as 0 for "never" or "once a year" (67.5 percent) and 1 for any higher frequency. Parental education is grouped into three bands: low (≤ 9 years, 50.2 percent), medium (about 12 years, 28.4 percent), and high (≥ 15 years, 21.4 percent). By BIC, the model without interactions fits better (11,574 versus 11,604), consistent with the categorized education scale washing out the interaction seen in Model 2.

Log-odds of higher participation (base: low education, rural):

\[ \ln\frac{P}{1-P} = -1.334 + 0.369\,\text{edu}_{\text{mid}} + 0.655\,\text{edu}_{\text{high}} + 0.658\,\text{semi} + 0.816\,\text{urban} + 0.168\,\text{female} - 0.337\,\text{grade9} - 0.279\,\text{siblings} + 0.037\,\text{intact} \]

and the predicted probability follows the logistic transform:

\[ P(\text{higher}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\eta}}, \qquad \eta = \ln\frac{P}{1-P} \]

Higher parental education, urban residence, being female, an earlier grade, and an intact home all raise the probability of higher participation.

7. Multinomial Logistic Model

Participation is split three ways: low ("never" or "once a year," 67.5 percent), moderate ("once every half year"), and high ("once a month" or more). The covariates match the binary model, and again BIC prefers the model without interactions (13,334 versus 13,395). The two latent contrasts, relative to low participation, are:

\[ \begin{aligned} Z_1 &= -1.968 + 0.447\,\text{edu}_{\text{mid}} + 0.817\,\text{edu}_{\text{high}} + 0.198\,\text{female} - 0.298\,\text{grade9} - 0.426\,\text{siblings} + 0.259\,\text{intact} + 0.592\,\text{semi} + 0.661\,\text{urban} \\[4pt] Z_2 &= -2.466 + 0.218\,\text{edu}_{\text{mid}} + 0.367\,\text{edu}_{\text{high}} + 0.042\,\text{female} - 0.424\,\text{grade9} - 0.106\,\text{siblings} + 0.302\,\text{intact} + 0.585\,\text{semi} + 0.981\,\text{urban} \end{aligned} \]

where \(Z_1\) is moderate-versus-low and \(Z_2\) is high-versus-low. The predicted probabilities are the softmax of these contrasts:

\[ P(\text{low}) = \frac{1}{1+e^{Z_1}+e^{Z_2}}, \quad P(\text{moderate}) = \frac{e^{Z_1}}{1+e^{Z_1}+e^{Z_2}}, \quad P(\text{high}) = \frac{e^{Z_2}}{1+e^{Z_1}+e^{Z_2}} \]

The picture is consistent across all three models: parental education, urban residence, being female, an earlier grade, and an intact home are associated with higher participation.

8. Reflection on Method

Two methodological points deserve their own section, because each is a finding in its own right.

The interaction vanished because of categorization, not cleaning. The education-by-place interaction is significant in the OLS, then disappears in the logistic models. It is tempting to blame data cleaning, but the data are clear once the cause is isolated. Holding the cleaned sample and the outcome fixed and varying only how finely education is measured:

ModelEducation scaleInteraction with urbanicityVerdict
OLScontinuous (years)F(2, 9751) = 7.07, p = 0.0009significant
OLS3 categoriesF(4, 9748) = 1.85, p = 0.12not significant
Binary logitcontinuous (years)LR χ²(2) = 13.6, p = 0.001significant
Binary logit3 categoriesdropped by BICnot significant

The interaction survives at the continuous scale in both model families and dies only when education is collapsed into three bands. Collapsing education compresses exactly the variation that interacts with place, so the right reading is that categorization, not cleaning, obscured a real relationship. The continuous specification should be preferred when the interaction is of interest.

Cleaning still cost representativeness. That said, adjusting for family composition dropped roughly 10,000 cases, and the rural share fell from 41 percent to 35.6 percent. Those drops are unlikely to be random: migrant and left-behind families, who are disproportionately rural, are exactly the ones with incomplete family-composition records. So the cleaned sample buys internal consistency at the price of external validity, and the estimates here may understate rural disadvantage. This is a separate limitation from the interaction point above, and both should be kept in view.

A note on the intact-home and sibling variables. As coded, intact home counts both parents present with no co-resident sibling, which makes it correlate strongly and negatively with has_siblings (r = −0.71): an only child with both parents is almost always coded intact. The two variables therefore share variance, and the intact-home coefficient is small and only marginally significant (p = 0.07). Defining the intact home on parental presence alone, regardless of siblings (82 percent of the sample), decorrelates the two (r = −0.11) and yields a clean, significant estimate for each: an intact-home premium of about +0.19 and a sibling penalty of about −0.16 on the 1 to 6 scale. The parental-presence definition is the cleaner specification, and a revision should adopt it.

9. Conclusion

Using CEPS data, this study asks how parental education and place shape children's cultural participation in China. Across all three models, parental education and urbanicity are the most consistent predictors. Higher parental schooling and urban residence are associated with significantly more frequent museum and cultural visits, even after adjusting for gender, grade, and household structure, and the education-by-place interaction shows that schooling matters most where place matters least, partly closing the rural gap rather than widening it.

The models also agree that an earlier grade, an intact two-parent home, and urban residence are associated with higher participation. The sibling and intact-home effects are entangled in the current coding and are cleaner under the parental-presence definition discussed above, but the direction is stable.

Two limitations bound these claims. First, the outcome bundles museums with zoos and science centers, so it measures informal cultural participation broadly rather than museum attendance alone. Second, the study omits school-organized visits, which may matter most in less advantaged areas where family-initiated visits are rare. Both would tend to make these estimates conservative for museums proper.

Despite a decade of policy aimed at opening museums to everyone, participation remains tied to family background and place. As museums keep expanding as sites of learning and identity, narrowing these gaps will take more than free admission: lighter homework, support for children in single-parent or non-parental care, simpler non-digital booking, and funding for cultural programs in disadvantaged areas all deserve attention.
