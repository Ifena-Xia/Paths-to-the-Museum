1. Why Museum Participation Matters

Museum participation is not simply a matter of leisure — it is a form of learning, cultural identification, and long-term civic development. Sandell & Zimmerman (2017) and Lin et al. (2023) highlight the potential of museum environments to shape reflective teaching and interactive learning. Holmes (2011) and Acosta & Haden (2022) show that even brief museum-based tinkering experiences can generate gains in science learning and reflective thinking, particularly when scaffolded by facilitation and parents' own education. Early museum visits foster children's cognitive, creative, and social capacities (Cronin et al., 2020), and support learning transfer into the home environment, where caregivers mediate and extend the experience. Temple and Gan (2023) demonstrate that early involvement in cultural activities significantly predicts lifelong engagement, especially among men. Paredes (2016) illustrates that immigrant identity may inspire engagement with museums as spaces for cultural validation and civic inclusion. In this way, museums act not only as educational tools but as vehicles of intergenerational transmission.

More broadly, participation in cultural life carries long-term implications for wellbeing. Løkken et al. (2020) find that cultural engagement, including receptive activities like museum visits, is associated with reduced all-cause mortality.

2. What Shapes Participation — and What Restricts It

Despite their growing visibility as educational environments, museums remain unequally accessed and unevenly experienced. Across contexts, one predictor stands out: education — especially parental education — emerges as the most consistent determinant of cultural participation (Falk & Katz-Gerro, 2016; Zieba, 2017). Tertiary education increases both the probability and frequency of attendance (Muñiz et al., 2017; Suarez-Fernandez et al., 2020; Bone et al., 2021), often surpassing income or occupational class in explanatory power (Haag & Specht, 2022; Fiorillo & Ofria, 2024). Cultural engagement is shaped not only by access to resources, but by the ability to navigate and decode highbrow spaces (Brida et al., 2016). This cultural capital, deeply embedded in class-based educational trajectories, is transmitted through families over time (Váradi & Józsa, 2023; Crispin & Beck, 2025).

Studies have extended this insight to children's participation in cultural life. Mak and Fancourt (2021) show that out-of-school participation in arts and cultural activities is strongly predicted by parents' own cultural engagement, neighborhood deprivation, and socioeconomic status. While school-based programs may flatten inequality temporarily, family influence remains the dominant force in long-term participation (Temple & Gan, 2023). Borrione et al. (2021) further observe that frequent-attending families tend to value museum staff expertise, educational content, and learning outcomes, while infrequent-attending families prioritize rest areas, multimedia, and play — suggesting that class-based orientations influence not just whether, but how families engage with museums.

Geography compounds these divides. Spatial barriers, including rural residence, neighborhood deprivation, and infrastructure scarcity, affect both access and perceived cultural belonging (Mak et al., 2021; Rodríguez-Puello & Iturra, 2024). Intra-household studies like Ateca-Amestoy and Ugidos (2021) reveal that women's participation in cultural life is further shaped by childcare responsibilities and mobility constraints, suggesting that structural inequalities — not just preferences — govern family engagement with museums. Olivares and Piatak (2022) add a racial lens, showing that Black Americans are significantly less likely to attend museums than white peers, even after controlling for education and income. Yet Latinx and Black respondents are more likely to cite cultural heritage as a motivation for attendance, emphasizing the importance of representation and institutional culture.

At the macro level, Ateca-Amestoy & Prieto-Rodriguez (2024) confirm that education explains more inequality in cultural participation than race or geography. Importantly, these inequalities are mirrored in digital spaces, undermining the idea that online access resolves exclusion. Instead, cultural engagement — whether live or digital — remains clustered among the already-engaged.

3. The Case of China: Rapid Growth, Persistent Inequality

In China, museum development has accelerated rapidly (Zhang & Courty, 2021). Admission has been widely waived, public programming has expanded, and cultural heritage is increasingly tied to state identity (Lu, 2014; Gideon, 2019; Lin, 2023, 2024). Yet who accesses museums remains sharply unequal. Rao et al. (2022) show that maternal education is a significant predictor of children's early development and cognitive stimulation — particularly in urban settings. Li (2024) observes that even where access is nominally free, high-quality engagement is constrained by design. Limited reservation quotas, underdeveloped signage, digital-first booking systems, and insufficiently inclusive programming impede access — especially for families with lower levels of education or digital literacy. Zhao et al. (2024) further note that recognition of museums' public value is most common among highly educated users and professionals, raising the question of whether museum participation is equitably distributed across social groups.

These barriers are particularly urgent given museums' dual status in China as both state-sanctioned patriotic spaces and emerging sites of experiential learning and social mobility. Yet large-scale quantitative studies of children's museum attendance remain rare. While many studies investigate cultural nationalism, digital communication, or museum governance (Bolo & Zhang, 2017), few explore how intergenerational inequality — especially via parental education — shapes actual access to cultural institutions.

4. Data Processing and Analysis

This study draws on data from the China Education Panel Survey (CEPS), which began in the 2013–2014 academic year with two baseline cohorts: 7th grade and 9th grade. Based on stratified sampling that accounted for the average education level of the population and the proportion of the floating population, CEPS selected 28 county-level units across China. Within these, 112 schools and 438 classes were randomly selected, yielding data from 19,487 students. After removing cases with missing or inconsistent information, the analytic sample includes 9,761 families with complete, internally consistent records.

Due to China's hukou (household registration) system, it is common for children to remain in registered hometowns while parents migrate for work. Because this left-behind arrangement is underreported in the survey (only 3,299 families formally noted it), sibling counts reported by students are used regardless of co-residence.

Key variables include:

  1. Cultural participation frequency: parental responses to "Visiting museums, zoos, science museums, etc." over the past year, on a six-point scale from "Never" to "More than once a week."
  2. Parental education: the higher attainment of mother or father, converted to years of schooling. Mean: 11.07 years (SD = 3.11).
  3. Gender, grade, and number of siblings: reported by students.
  4. Residential area (urbanicity): reported by parents, recoded to urban core, semi-urban, and rural.
  5. Living arrangement (standard_living): 1 if living with both parents (with or without grandparents), 0 otherwise.

5. OLS Model

Participation frequency was recoded from six ordered categories into a numeric scale from 1 to 6. Parental education was expressed as years of schooling. Several categorical variables were recoded as binary dummies: female (1 = female), grade9 (1 = ninth grade), has_siblings (1 = one or more siblings; 49.7% of sample), and standard_living (1 = both parents present, with or without grandparents; 48% of sample). Urbanicity was recoded to three levels: urban core (36.3%), semi-urban (28.1%), and rural (35.6%).

Model 1 (no interaction terms):

frequency = 1.784 + 0.0578 parent_edu − 0.1233 semi_urban − 0.4987 rural
           + 0.0556 female − 0.1863 grade9 − 0.1222 has_siblings + 0.0622 standard_living

Given prior literature suggesting the relationship between parental education and participation may vary by region, interaction terms between parental education and urbanicity level were introduced. Model 2:

frequency = 2.006 + 0.0401 parent_edu − 0.4773 semi_urban − 0.9231 rural
           + 0.0293 parent_edu×semi_urban + 0.0391 parent_edu×rural
           + 0.0561 female − 0.1813 grade9 − 0.1251 has_siblings + 0.0655 standard_living

An F-test comparing the two models yielded a p-value of 0.0006, indicating that the interaction terms significantly improve fit. Model 2 is therefore preferred. The chart below illustrates predicted participation frequencies from Model 2 by parental education, urbanicity, sibling status, and grade. Urban levels 1, 2, and 3 denote urban core, semi-urban, and rural respectively; circle lines represent no siblings; triangle lines represent having siblings; solid lines represent grade 7 and dashed lines grade 9.

From Model 2, participation frequency decreases markedly as urbanicity declines. However, higher parental education partially compensates for this regional disadvantage, and its positive effect grows stronger in lower-urbanicity settings. Female students, 7th graders, only children, and those in standard family structures all show higher predicted participation.

6. Binary Logistic Model

The dependent variable was recoded into a binary outcome: "Never" and "Once a year" (67.5% of the sample) were coded as 0 (none or low participation); all higher frequencies were coded as 1 (higher participation). Parental education was grouped into three levels: low (junior high school and below, ≤9 years; 50.2%), medium (technical secondary, vocational, or senior high school, ~12 years; 28.3%), and high (junior college and above, ≥15 years; 21.4%).

8. Reflection on Interaction Terms

A particularly interesting finding is the disappearance of the interaction between parental education and living area after cleaning and variable recoding. Before adjusting for family composition — which resulted in the loss of approximately 10,000 cases — and before collapsing parental education into three broad categories, the interaction between rural status and parental education remained salient across OLS and logistic models alike.

After cleaning, the rural proportion dropped from 41% to 35.6%, and categorical aggregation of educational levels reduced variability. Both factors likely contributed to the loss of statistical significance for the interaction term. This reflects how data cleaning and variable transformation can sometimes obscure meaningful relational structures in social data — a methodological caution worth noting for future research using large survey datasets.

9. Conclusion

This study examines how parental education and geographic location shape children's cultural participation in China, using CEPS survey data. Consistent with prior research, parental education and living area emerge as the most cogent predictors of participation across all models. Higher educational attainment among parents and urban residence significantly increase children's frequency of museum and cultural visits, even after controlling for gender, grade, and household structure.

OLS and logistic regressions converge on a further finding: being in a lower grade, having no siblings, living with both parents or with parents and grandparents, and residing in an urban area all elevate participation likelihoods. Interaction terms between parental education and urbanicity — initially salient in the full dataset — disappeared after dataset cleaning and variable categorization, suggesting that reduced rural representation and the collapse of educational levels into broad categories may obscure subtler correlations between parental education and region.

The biggest limitation of this study is its omission of school-organized museum visits, which may contribute substantially to children's cultural exposure, particularly in less advantaged areas where family-initiated visits are rare.

Overall, despite policy efforts to democratize museum access, cultural participation remains closely tied to family background and spatial advantage. As museums continue to expand as sites of learning and identity-building, addressing these underlying disparities — through more inclusive design, outreach, and programming — is critical to ensuring equitable access. Beyond eliminating admission fees, measures such as reducing course load, supporting children from single-parent or non-parental care arrangements, and financing cultural programs in disadvantaged areas deserve further attention.
